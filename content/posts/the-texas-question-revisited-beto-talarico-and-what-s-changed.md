Seven years ago I wrote a short essay on Beto O'Rourke and his chances in the Democratic Presidential Primaries. A lot has changed in those seven years, and I wrote that piece before I'd even attended university, let alone studied politics seriously. In that aforementioned essay, I suggested that despite Beto lagging behind the leaders in the polls, his charisma - the thing that made him such a unique and interesting candidate - might be enough to earn some support in the first televised debate of 2019. It had almost worked for him in his Senate race against Ted Cruz, after all. However, several election cycles later, Beto has all but disappeared from public politics and Texas has remained reliably Republican. But Democrats hoping for their first statewide win since 1994 have a new candidate to keep an eye on.

Enter James Talarico, a state representative from Texas' 50th district and former educator. On the surface, the comparisons between Beto and Talarico are easy to make - a young and charismatic Democrat who's currently punching beyond his weight against established Republican senators. But where seven years ago I was somewhat bullish about the idea that Beto's charisma would be enough to make a difference, the political landscape in 2026 demands more than just smooth words and good vibes. Talarico has already laid the groundwork for a strong, issue-focused campaign, and the more you look at the polling and his strategy, the clearer it becomes that the comparison to Beto is merely a cosmetic one.

### Why does this even matter?

Texas is a state the Democrats have struggled in for decades. However in recent years (and depending on who you asked), Texas had been seen as trending towards a "purple" state that Democrats could be competitive in. Yet election after election, it has failed to materialise. The feeling of a "perpetual phantom hope that this will be the cycle that finally breaks the fever" (Elliot, TIME, 2025) has haunted Democratic candidates, and raised concerns over spending big in unwinnable states. But in an electoral map where swing states are becoming fewer in number and the electorates more centralised in GOP-leaning states, Democrats need to find a breakthrough in order to have a chance at future electoral success. Simply put, the Democrats need to be competitive in a state like Texas or Florida, and change this narrative that puts them on the back foot. A statewide win in Texas - not to mention in a midterm election where Democratic Senate seats are under threat - would allow for them to break this cycle.
